## 3. What went well (top themes)

### 3.1 The single-choke-point design (2-3) turned 2-4's masking into one edit — a bet that paid off exactly as designed

This is the standout engineering win of the epic, and it is a *design-for-the-future-story* win. In 2-3, all three value-display surfaces — the human `config get` table, `config get --json`, and the persisted `effective-config.json` snapshot — were deliberately routed through **one** `ResolvedValue::display()` choke point, even though 2-3 did no masking at all. The 2-3 commit body states the intent in plain terms: *"so story 2-4 adds secret masking in a single place."*

Then 2-4 collected on it: masking hooked that one choke point and **every** display surface masked at once. The no-leak test matrix could then prove, at four levels (type/engine/registry/CLI), that a sentinel secret reaches the adapter's native env but appears in *none* of the snapshot, `--json`, human table, or any Agent-Home file — because there was structurally only one place a value could be rendered.

> Charlie (Senior Dev): "This is the good kind of coupling. 2-3 paid a small tax — 'route everything through this one function even though you don't need it yet' — to make 2-4 a single edit *and* make 2-4 auditable. When the adversarial reviewer went looking for leak surfaces, the answer to 'where can a value be printed?' was 'exactly one function.' You cannot audit a diffuse design that cheaply. This is the 1-7 uninhabited-enum move again: make the safe thing the *only* thing the structure permits."
>
> Winston (Architect): "It's the honesty thread from Epic 1 continuing to mature — Epic 1 pushed honesty into the type system (uninhabited `MeteringSeed`); Epic 2 pushed *leak-prevention* into topology (one choke point). Same principle: don't rely on every call site remembering to do the right thing; make the structure only permit the right thing."

### 3.2 The TWO-parallel-review pattern on the security-sensitive story caught what a single review would miss

2-4 (secrets) was reviewed by **two reviewers in parallel**: the primary `bmad-code-review` (APPROVED) and a *dedicated adversarial secret-leak audit* whose sole job was to find a cleartext escape. The primary approved the implementation on its merits; the adversarial pass independently swept every observable surface, found them **airtight**, and surfaced the one thing a correctness-focused review would likely have passed over: the `Flag`→argv exposure (a secret on a command-line flag is visible cross-user via `ps`). That became AI-33, was documented in-story, and is now a known boundary rather than a latent surprise.

> Dana (QA): "A single reviewer asks 'is this correct?' An adversarial reviewer asks 'how do I get the secret out?' Those find different bugs. The argv finding isn't a bug in the masking — the masking is perfect — it's a bug in the *threat model* that only a 'how do I exfiltrate' mindset surfaces. For a secrets story, running both in parallel is the right cost."
>
> Charlie (Senior Dev): "And note it mirrors the Epic 1 review lesson from a different angle. In Epic 1 the win was a review that was *falsifiable both ways* (refuted two false HIGHs, confirmed two real ones). Here the win is review *diversity*: two reviewers with different mandates on the same diff. Both lessons say the same meta-thing — a review process is only worth its cost if it can surprise you."

### 3.3 Per-epic PRs into `main` finally ran real 3-OS CI — and that is a feature, not a bug

Islam's strategy of landing each epic as one squashed PR into `main` (Epic 1 = #100, Epic 2 = #102) gave clean, epic-sized landings *and* — crucially — was the **first time Epic 2 code ran on the full macOS + Windows + Linux matrix**. The whole reason §4.3 (the `_live` fragility) got caught at all is that the epic PR ran the matrix. So while §4.3 is a "we found it late" lesson, the *mechanism that found it* is this one. Per-epic PRs are the safety net that turned local-only blind spots into visible, fixable CI failures before merge.

> Alice (Product Owner): "I want to defend the per-epic-PR call explicitly, because §4.3 could read as an argument against it. It isn't. Without the epic PR, those four cross-OS failures would have shipped to `main` undetected and surfaced for a *contributor*, not for us. The PR did its job. The lesson in §4.3 is 'run the matrix even *earlier*,' not 'stop running it at the PR.'"

### 3.4 Zero-new-dependency discipline on a security primitive

2-4 introduced `SecretString` as a **hand-rolled newtype** — redacting `Display`/`Debug`, cleartext only through `expose_secret()`, no `Serialize` — rather than pulling in the obvious `secrecy` crate. The entire epic added exactly **one** runtime dependency (`toml`, workspace-pinned), for the config model. For a foundational engine that will be embedded (Epic 7), keeping the secret-handling primitive dependency-free and auditable in-tree is the conservative, correct call.

> Winston (Architect): "A secret type is exactly the kind of thing you want to *own*. It's ~40 lines, it's security-critical, and a supply-chain surprise in a `secrecy`-shaped dependency would be a bad day. Hand-rolling it means the adversarial auditor could read the whole thing. That's the right trade for this crate."

**Honorable mentions:** the additive `CONTRACT_VERSION` bump (0.1.0→0.2.0 — a manifest with no `[config]` is simply an empty mapping, so no existing adapter breaks); path-safety hardened on *both* the manifest and native File-target paths (no Agent-Home escape, AD-6); `set_dotted` failing *closed* on a non-table intermediate (`WriteShapeConflict`) rather than clobbering — a data-loss guard that was itself a review find in 2-1.

## 4. What was hard / what to improve (top themes)

*(These four are the heart of this retrospective. Three are process learnings about how we verify; one is a repo-shape friction.)*

### 4.1 The 2-1 resolver was REWRITTEN after review — a first-pass design that was subtly wrong on shape collisions and data loss

2-1 was the epic's foundation, and its first implementation drew a **CHANGES-REQUIRED** review. Two of the findings were not cosmetic — they were correctness holes in the merge semantics:

- a **shape-collision** bug (a stronger *subtree* meeting a weaker *leaf*, or vice versa, was not handled such that siblings survived and the tree stayed self-consistent); and
- a **`set_dotted` data-loss** bug (writing a dotted key through a non-table intermediate could clobber an existing value instead of failing closed).

The response was the right one but expensive: the resolver was **rewritten** — re-derived around the `MergedNode` tree-merge-then-flatten-once model — and put through a **second, adversarial** review pass, which independently re-derived the new resolver as correct (no new bug introduced) and APPROVED. The final design is stronger for it (the "never self-contradictory" property is now structural), but the first pass shipped into review with a design flaw that a merge-semantics thought-experiment — or a property test over overlapping leaf/subtree layers — would have caught before the review round.

**Lesson:** for a *foundational, algorithm-shaped* story (a resolver, a merge, a state machine), the highest-leverage pre-review artifact is an **explicit enumeration of the shape/collision cases** (leaf-vs-leaf, leaf-vs-subtree, subtree-vs-leaf, subtree-vs-subtree) with a test per case — *before* the first review, not after the rewrite. The rewrite was healthy (the process caught it, exactly as designed), but the *class* of bug (structural merge collisions) is one we can front-load a checklist for.

> Charlie (Senior Dev): "I don't want this read as 'the first attempt was bad.' A layered-config merge with subtree masking is genuinely one of the trickier small algorithms — the leaf-vs-subtree cases are where every config system has bled. The lesson is that *this specific shape* of problem has a *known* enumeration of hard cases, and for the next algorithm-shaped story (the metering accumulator in Epic 3 is one) we should write that enumeration down as tests on day one. The review is the backstop, not the design tool."
>
> Winston (Architect): "The reassuring part is the *second* review independently re-derived the rewrite as correct rather than just diffing it. A rewrite reviewed by 'looks different, looks fine' would scare me. A rewrite reviewed by re-derivation is the spine working."

### 4.2 Coverage-runner OOM (#101 / AI-31) — UNRESOLVED after FIVE fixes; both epics merged over a red gate

This is the single hardest and most instructive thread of the epic, and it is **still open**. The CI `coverage` job repeatedly died on the 7 GB hosted runner — the runner "loses communication … starves it for CPU/Memory," an OOM that **drops the job with no log**, ~49 minutes in. Five fixes were attempted, each peeling a layer and each costing a *blind ~50-minute cycle* because the failure produces no diagnostic:

1. **Cache the tarpaulin binary** — hypothesis: install cost. *Wrong layer* — the install was never the bottleneck.
2. **`--engine llvm`** — hypothesis: execution instrumentation cost. *Wrong layer* — the cost is the **compile**, not execution.
3. **A dedicated `-cargo-coverage-` cache key** — so the instrumented `target/` persists. **Real progress**: this made the *cold instrumented build complete* for the first time. But then the job still died later.
4. **Serial tests (`RUST_TEST_THREADS=1`) + 8 GB swap** — hypothesis: test-time parallel memory. *Didn't move the failure point* — proving it is **not** parallel test execution.
5. **Reclaim ~30 GB disk on `/` (unused SDKs) + `CARGO_PROFILE_DEV_DEBUG=1`** (line-tables-only debuginfo) — attacking both RAM (smaller instrumented binaries) and disk (the earlier swap was on `/mnt` and never relieved `/`, where the instrumented `target/` + profraw live). **Still OOM.**

**Root cause (now understood):** the *instrumented* build of this workspace exceeds the free runner's combined RAM+disk budget. It is not install cost, not execution parallelism, not `/mnt` swap — it is the peak footprint of compiling the coverage-instrumented binaries. Both **Epic 1 (#100)** and **Epic 2 (#102)** were ultimately **admin-merged over the red coverage check** (Islam-authorized), with coverage proven ≥95% *locally* each time.

**Lesson (territory the whole team flagged):** a "coverage must be green" gate that is undermined by an *infra limit the code cannot satisfy on the given runner* is not actually gating — it is a red light everyone has learned to drive through, and "learning to merge over a red check" is a dangerous habit to build (it erodes the signal of *every* red check). #101 needs a real fix: a **larger-RAM runner** (cleanest — Islam's cost call) or a **per-crate tarpaulin split** (so no single job holds the whole instrumented workspace in memory), possibly plus `codegen-units`/`CARGO_BUILD_JOBS` tuning. The blind-cycle cost also argues for reproducing the OOM *locally under a constrained cgroup* (memory-capped `docker run`) so the next fix is diagnosed in minutes, not in 50-minute CI roulette.

> Dana (QA): "The scariest artifact of this whole epic is not a bug — it's that we now have *institutional muscle memory for merging over a red check*. That's how a real regression eventually walks in behind a coverage OOM nobody reads anymore. We must either fix #101 or, if the fix is weeks out, explicitly downgrade the gate to non-blocking-with-a-local-proof-requirement so a red coverage check means something again."
>
> Charlie (Senior Dev): "Five fixes, each a ~50-minute blind cycle, because the runner dies *without a log*. The meta-lesson is: when a CI failure gives no diagnostic, stop iterating in CI. Reproduce it locally under the same memory ceiling (a cgroup / memory-capped container) and you turn a 50-minute coin-flip into a 5-minute measurement. We diagnosed the *layers* correctly in the end, but we paid CI-latency prices for local-measurable facts."
>
> Islam (Project Lead): *[decision territory — this is mine: a larger-RAM coverage runner is a cost line I need to approve, vs. investing the eng time in a per-crate split that keeps us on the free runner. Either unblocks the gate; the split also speeds every future coverage run. Leaning split + revisit runner if the split alone doesn't fit.]*

### 4.3 Cross-OS CI surfaced LATE — the `_live` tests were verified macOS-only and 4 failed at once on the epic PR

Epic 2's `_live` tests — which spawn the *real* `fake_agent` and poll for a `--dump` file it writes — were developed and verified **local-only on macOS** and never touched the 3-OS matrix until the **epic PR (#102)**. The matrix then surfaced **four failing tests at once**:

- the CLI `secret_reaches_the_adapter_but_never_leaks_and_reveal_shows_it`, and
- three supervisor `_live` tests (`manifest_start_delivers_agent_pass_through_verbatim_live`, `manifest_start_maps_model_to_the_declared_flag_target_live`, `secret_leaf_delivers_cleartext_to_the_adapter_but_masks_snapshot_and_events`).

The failures were a mix of **macOS timing/survival** (a loaded runner's surviving agent doesn't write the dump within the deadline — and for the CLI test, the one-shot `kt agent start` leaves no observable running agent on macOS CI at all) and **Windows kill-on-close** (`JOB_OBJECT_LIMIT_KILL_ON_JOB_CLOSE` kills the child the instant `kt` exits, so the dump is never written). The fix landed in-PR across three commits (progressively: gate off Windows + raise the deadline → still failed on macOS → **gate Linux-only**), tracked as **AI-35**. The delivery logic these tests prove is OS-agnostic engine code, covered on Linux (+ tarpaulin) and by Epic 1's OS-specific spawn/backend tests on all three OSes — so Linux-only is *defensible*, but it is a **coverage retreat** logged as debt, not a clean win.

**Lesson:** **local-only verification systematically misses cross-OS (and coverage) issues** — this is the *same class* of blind spot as §4.2, just on a different axis. Waiting until the epic PR to run the matrix meant discovering four failures simultaneously, under merge pressure, and resolving them by *gating tests off* rather than by making them robust. Run the 3-OS matrix **earlier** — per-story, or at least periodically on the working branch (a manually-triggered or nightly matrix run on `feat/*`) — so cross-OS failures surface one at a time, on the story that introduced them, with time to make them *robust* rather than *gated*.

> Dana (QA): "Two failures showing up on the story that caused them is a debuggable Tuesday. Four failures showing up together on the epic PR under merge pressure is a fire, and fires get put out by *disabling the smoke detector* — which is exactly what 'gate it Linux-only' is. The tests aren't wrong; the *timing of when we ran them* forced the cheapest possible fix instead of the right one."
>
> Amelia (Developer): "And note the through-line with 4.2: both are 'local-only verification hid a truth until the last possible moment.' Coverage and cross-OS are the two things a macOS dev box *cannot* tell you. We should treat 'has this run on the matrix?' and 'has coverage run in CI?' as first-class per-story questions, not epic-PR surprises."

### 4.4 The `_live` test *pattern itself* is fragile — spawn-an-agent-and-watch-a-file is inherently racy and OS-dependent

Distinct from *when* we ran them (§4.3): the `_live` tests' **shape** is the deeper problem. "Spawn a real agent, then poll for a file it writes" couples the test to (a) process spawn latency (unbounded on a loaded runner), (b) the agent surviving long enough to write (which the one-shot `kt` lifetime and Windows kill-on-close actively fight), and (c) OS-specific binary naming (`fake_agent` vs `fake_agent.exe`). Every one of the §4.3 failures traces to one of these three. The robust pattern already exists in the codebase: Epic 1's **surviving-engine harness** (a subprocess that keeps the engine — and thus the adopted child — alive) and/or a **readiness signal** from the agent instead of a fixed dump-timeout poll. AI-35 explicitly lists the fix: `.exe` handling, a readiness signal or adaptive timeout, and routing the CLI test through the surviving-engine harness with env injection for the resolver.

**Lesson:** file-appearance polling is a **test smell** for anything that spawns a real process. Prefer a **readiness handshake** (the agent signals "I'm up and have written X") over a wall-clock deadline, and prefer the surviving-engine harness over a one-shot `kt` whenever the assertion needs the agent to still be alive after `kt` returns. This is a reusable pattern decision, not a one-off fix — Epic 6 (conformance) will spawn many real agents and should adopt the robust pattern from the start.

> Charlie (Senior Dev): "A test that polls for a file with a timeout is really asserting 'the machine is fast enough today.' That's not a property of our code — it's a property of the runner's load. The surviving-engine harness converts 'wait and hope' into 'wait for an explicit signal,' which is deterministic. We already *have* the pattern from Epic 1; the `_live` tests just predate our adoption of it."

### 4.5 The squash-only repo made the Epic-2 rebase non-trivial

Because both epics land as *single squashed commits*, the Epic-2 branch (developed on `feat/agent-runner-pivot` against pre-squash Epic 1) had to be **rebased `--onto` the squashed Epic 1** (`5506c4f`) after #100 merged — the individual Epic-2 story commits were replayed onto a base whose history had collapsed to one commit, with one commit (`db01fad`) auto-dropped as already-upstream. It was handled cleanly (backup ref `backup/feat-pre-rebase` kept, `--locked` check green), but it was **avoidable friction**: squash-merging the base epic means the child epic's branch can't fast-forward and must be surgically re-based.

**Lesson:** this is an inherent tension of "squash every epic PR" + "develop the next epic on a long-lived branch off the *pre-squash* base." It's manageable (the `git rebase --onto <squash> <old-base>` recipe works and is now proven), but it will recur every epic boundary. Two mitigations worth a decision: (a) **document the `rebase --onto` recipe** as the standard epic-boundary procedure (it's currently tribal knowledge in a `sprint-status` note), or (b) **branch the next epic off the squashed `main`** rather than the pre-squash feature branch, so no rebase is needed.

> Winston (Architect): "This is a pure workflow cost, not an engineering one, but it's a *recurring* one — it'll happen at every single epic boundary for the life of the project. A three-line documented recipe (or the discipline of always branching the next epic off merged `main`) makes it a non-event instead of a per-epic puzzle."
