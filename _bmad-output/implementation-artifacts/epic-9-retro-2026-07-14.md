## 3. What went well (top themes)

### 3.1 The first fully-green-CI epic in the project's history — and it's traceable to a specific prior fix, not luck

Epics 1, 2, and 3 each carried an explicit "merged over red" asterisk in their retros — coverage OOM, flaky Windows/ubuntu legs, or both. Epic 9's PR #114 ran 11 CI jobs (`boundary`, `semver`, `fmt`, `test` on ubuntu/macos/windows, `build`, `docs`, `msrv`, `clippy`, `coverage`) across all three of its commits, and every single one came back green. This isn't an accident of a smaller epic: PR #108 (`8d975e7`, cargo-nextest adoption + the Linux-in-CI adoption-test skip) landed **the same day, immediately before** Epic 9's branch opened, which is exactly what AI-50 and AI-53 (Epic 3's two highest-severity action items) asked for.

> Charlie (Senior Dev): "This is the payoff of not treating AI-50/53 as 'recommendations' — the Epic 3 retro explicitly said those two needed to be *hard prerequisites*, not suggestions that slide. They got fixed *before* Epic 9 started, and Epic 9 is the proof: three-for-three green, first time ever."

### 3.2 Review intensity matched each story's actual risk shape, not a one-size policy

- **9-1** (the deletion) got the heaviest treatment: a primary correctness/completeness pass **plus** a dedicated adversarial "excision-safety" audit that built both the before/after commits, diffed live `kt agent --help`/`kt self-update --help` byte-for-byte against baseline, and grepped for any feature-gated or doctest survivor of the 17 removed symbols. Both passes returned APPROVED with only LOW, already-deferred nits (the dangling workspace deps, later cleaned in 9-2; the stale `check_docs.py` allowlist, later tightened in 9-3).
- **9-2** (the rebrand + version bump) got a single, proportionate pass — appropriate to its lower code risk, and it specifically verified the traps the story itself had pre-identified (rebrand text checked verbatim against the live README/GitHub description; the canonical-surface guard proven **non-vacuous** via a live smoke test, not just a passing assertion; the version bump confirmed isolated to `workspace.package` alone).
- **9-3** split itself in two by who actually needed to be in the loop: Part A (the mechanical `docs/architecture.md` rewrite + `lockfile.md` retirement + `check_docs.py` tightening) needed no LLM review — it's gated by the same automated doc-lint that would have failed the build if it were wrong. Part B (the AD-16/Epic-8 re-scope) is a **product/architecture judgment call**, and the right check for that isn't an adversarial code review, it's a human ratifying a specific, bounded choice — which is what happened (§3.5).

> Dana (QA): "None of the three reviews this epic found a real bug the way Epic 3's adversarial passes did five-for-five. I don't read that as the reviews being weaker — a clean, mechanical, well-scoped deletion *should* review clean. The interesting signal isn't 'no bugs found,' it's that the *shape* of scrutiny tracked the *shape* of risk: heaviest on the irreversible deletion, lightest on the docs, human-only on the judgment call."

### 3.3 The 9-1 → 9-2 seam was pre-planned in writing, not improvised

9-1's own Dev Notes contain a section titled "The 9-1 ↔ 9-2 coupling" that explicitly anticipates the exact test-assertion split 9-2 would need to author (9-1 makes the minimal green-keeping trim; 9-2 adds the *positive* "new surface present" and *negative* "retired names absent" assertions) — including calling out, in advance, the recursive-`find_subcommand` trap that would have false-failed against the live `kt agent list`/`show`. 9-2 built on exactly that seam with zero rediscovery cost.

### 3.4 A forced mid-story deviation was surfaced, scoped correctly, and documented — not silently absorbed or scope-crept

9-1's story text listed `ui.rs` under **KEEP, no changes**. Mid-execution, `clippy -D warnings` (one of the story's own gates) surfaced 17 dead skill-manager-only helpers in `ui.rs`, orphaned by the excision, plus a now-unused `indicatif` dependency the story had also listed under KEEP. This is exactly the kind of "the story boundary discovers something the plan didn't see" moment that can go two ways — the dev agent removed the dead code (not `#[allow(dead_code)]`, which would have left dead skill-manager code in a story whose entire point was removing dead skill-manager code) and logged it explicitly as "Deviation 1" with its full reasoning, rather than either silently expanding the story's stated scope or quietly suppressing the warning.

> Charlie (Senior Dev): "This is the good version of scope pressure. The gate — clippy, not a person — forced the discovery. The fix stayed inside `crates/kt`, the same blast radius the story already owned. And it's written down as a deviation with a reason, so a reviewer or a future reader isn't left guessing why `ui.rs` changed when the plan said it wouldn't. That's the difference between a story boundary holding under pressure and a story boundary being fiction."

### 3.5 The draft-then-ratify pattern for the AD-16/Epic-8 correction is a clean, reusable shape

Story 9-3 Part B is an architecture/product decision (re-scoping Story 8-1's premise, marking 8-4 superseded), and it was handled with a clear separation of authority: Winston drafted a **complete proposal touching zero real planning artifacts**, Islam picked from a small, concrete set of options via `AskUserQuestion` ("apply, and keep Story 8-1 as its own story rather than merging it into 8-2"), and only then was the ratified decision applied verbatim to `ARCHITECTURE-SPINE.md`, `epics.md`, and synced to GitHub issues #62/#95/#98/#99 (with dependency notes added to #96/#97). Nothing was applied before ratification; nothing needed redoing after it.

> Winston (Architect): "I'd name this pattern rather than let it stay implicit: *propose without touching, human picks from bounded options, apply verbatim.* It's the same shape as a sprint-change-proposal, just scoped to a single architecture decision instead of a whole epic. Worth writing down so the next AD correction doesn't have to reinvent the shape."

### 3.6 A real usage-limit exhaustion was survived cleanly — this is the resumable-agent architecture working under an actual interruption, not a drill

Around 08:56 on 2026-07-14, right after Story 9-2 landed and its review subagent was spawned, the top-level orchestrating session hit an account-level usage limit ("You've hit your session limit"). This froze **two things at once**: the top-level orchestrator itself, and the in-flight Story 9-2 review subagent, mid-verification. The session didn't resume until roughly ten hours later, at 19:06, when Islam came back and typed `/sprint-orchestrator - please continue`.

What happened on resume is the actual finding: the orchestrator re-derived its full situational state from durable artifacts alone — it re-checked PR #114's live CI status, recognized that a specific named subagent was still sitting mid-review, and re-briefed that subagent with the **exact point it had stopped at** ("right after confirming `check_docs.py` and `test_automation.py` both pass, and while about to verify internal crate versions stayed pinned + read the full test module for the canonical-surface guard"). The subagent picked up from that exact point and delivered its verdict (APPROVED-WITH-NITS) a few minutes later. No work was redone; nothing was re-verified that had already been verified.

> Amelia (Developer): "I want to be precise about what I'm claiming here, because it would be easy to oversell this. What I can show from the transcript is **one** exhaustion event that happened to freeze two things simultaneously — not a string of four separate incidents. See §4.3 for why that distinction matters. But the one event that's fully documented is a genuinely good result: a ten-hour real gap, zero rework, and the recovery worked *because* the state (task tracker, PR/CI status, a named resumable subagent) lived outside any single conversation, not inside it."

## 4. What was hard / what to improve (top themes)

### 4.1 Review-pass count for release-facing artifacts deserves the same "two-pass by default" bar Epic 3 set for money/ledger stories

Story 9-2 bumped the published semver (0.5.0 → 0.6.0), hand-authored new `CHANGELOG.md`/`RELEASE_NOTES.md` entries, and rewrote the crates.io `description`/`keywords` that ride along on the next `cargo publish`. None of that is a bug in the traditional sense, but it's exactly the class of change where a mistake is expensive to walk back once it's live on crates.io — a broken migration link, a keyword that fails `cargo publish --dry-run`, or a version bump that's semantically wrong for a breaking CLI change. Nothing went wrong here; the single review pass specifically verified the traps the story had pre-flagged. But the *policy* gap is real: Epic 3's §6.3 named "money, ledger, enforcement, network" as the standing trigger list for two-pass review by default. "Touches the public release surface" isn't on that list, and arguably should be — not because this story needed a second pass to catch something, but because the next one might, and there's no reason to wait for that to happen before adding the category.

### 4.2 A small tracker-authority split: `sprint-status.yaml`'s own Story 8-1 entry is stale even though the authoritative docs are correct

The AD-16/Epic-8 re-scope was applied correctly to `epics.md` (Story 8-1's full text, ACs, and title) and to GitHub issue #95 (title, body, both explicitly say "Rewritten 2026-07-14... premise changed from relocating existing code to building it fresh"). But `sprint-status.yaml` — the file this project treats as the single source of truth for "what's next" — still carries the key `8-1-relocate-the-install-lock-machinery-into-the-engine`, the exact premise Epic 9 just voided, with no inline note pointing at the rewrite. Anyone who opens Epic 8 by trusting the tracker over `epics.md` will start from the wrong premise. Small, mechanical, and easy to fix (AI-56) — but worth naming, because it's the same class of drift the project has flagged before (Epic 2's retro named "documentation debt slides"; this is a *tracker* version of the same failure mode).

### 4.3 The "4 review-cutoff usage-limit interruptions" framing handed into this retro doesn't hold up against the transcript — it was one event, and that's worth saying plainly

Going into this retrospective, the orchestrating session's own preparatory note (and the brief this retro was run from) described "4 review-cutoff usage-limit interruptions mid-epic." Checking the surviving session transcript directly: there is exactly **one** documented, explicit usage-limit exhaustion during Epic 9 (the 08:56–19:06 window in §3.6), and it froze two things simultaneously — the top-level orchestrator and the in-flight 9-2 review subagent — because both were running under the same account-level limit at the same moment. A second top-level "please continue" queue entry 36 seconds after the first, at 19:06, turns out to be a user-side interrupted-and-resubmitted duplicate (`"[Request interrupted by user]"` sits between them), not a second distinct cutoff.

> Amelia (Developer): "I want to flag this rather than quietly fix the framing and move on, because the instinct to just accept 'four' and write a slightly more dramatic retro was right there. The actual story — one real exhaustion, two things frozen by it, both recovered cleanly — is simpler *and* just as strong a positive finding as four would have been. It doesn't need inflating, and retros are exactly the place a miscounted fact should get caught before it calcifies into project lore."

This isn't logged as an action item (there's nothing for the team to *do* about a miscounted fact in a prompt), but it directly shapes how §3.6 and the action items in §8 are framed — as one well-evidenced recovery, not four.

### 4.4 Story 9-3 has no dedicated story file — a reasonable adaptation, but an inconsistent artifact trail worth a conscious decision

`_bmad-output/implementation-artifacts/` holds `9-1-*.md` and `9-2-*.md`, each with full Acceptance Criteria, Dev Agent Record, File List, and Change Log sections. There is no `9-3-*.md`. Story 9-3 went straight from the sprint-change-proposal's AC text to a direct commit (`fc53278`) plus an out-of-band ratification exchange for Part B. This looks like a deliberate and reasonable adaptation — 9-3 was explicitly called "architect-owned" from the moment it was proposed, and the standard create-story → dev-story pipeline doesn't model "half mechanical execution, half human-ratified proposal" cleanly. But it does mean Epic 9 ends with two stories carrying full BMAD paper trails and one that doesn't, which is worth being a **conscious** convention (architect-owned stories get a lighter-weight artifact, or none) rather than an emergent gap nobody decided on.

## 6. Strategic / forward-looking findings

### 6.1 The 9-1 → 9-2 → 9-3 decomposition was a good split — worth naming why, not just that it worked

Excise → rebrand → docs/re-scope turned out to be a decomposition where every boundary held: 9-1 stayed entirely inside `crates/kt` even under the pressure of an unplanned discovery (§3.4); 9-2's seam with 9-1 was pre-specified in writing before 9-2 started (§3.3); and 9-3 recognized mid-flight that it needed a *different* ownership and gating model (architect-owned, partly human-ratified) rather than force-fitting itself into the same shape as 9-1/9-2 (§4.4). The lesson to carry forward isn't "split deletion/rebrand/docs into three stories" as a template — it's that each story's boundary was defined by *who needs to be in the loop and what can gate it automatically*, and that held up even when reality (the dead `ui.rs` code, the architecture judgment call) didn't match the plan exactly.

### 6.2 Codify "full-matrix CI on every commit of a feature branch" as a written practice, not an emergent side effect

Now that #106 makes a full cross-OS + coverage run fast (single-digit minutes per leg, per the story records), running it on every commit is nearly free — and Epic 9 proves it catches nothing hides until the PR. Recommend this become an explicit, documented default (CONTRIBUTING.md / `docs/testing.md`) rather than something that happens to occur because an epic is small and CI happens to be fast this month. This is AI-54, and it's the concrete continuation of Epic 2's AI-37.

### 6.3 Extend the two-pass-review default to cover public release-surface changes

Epic 3's §6.3 named money/ledger/enforcement/network surfaces as the standing trigger for "primary + adversarial" review. Recommend adding: any change to published semver, `CHANGELOG.md`/`RELEASE_NOTES.md`, or crates.io package metadata. The asymmetry is the point — these are the artifacts hardest to quietly correct after the fact. This is AI-55.

### 6.4 Name and document the draft-then-ratify pattern for architecture-decision corrections

Per §3.5 — worth a short, reusable write-up (even a few lines in the architect-agent's playbook) so the next AD correction defaults to "propose with zero real files touched → human picks from bounded options → apply verbatim" rather than reinventing the shape or, worse, applying a plausible-looking correction before it's actually been ratified. This is AI-58.

### 6.5 GitHub issue #109 (adoption.rs x86 ubuntu CI deadlock) — confirmed correctly tracked, out of Epic 9's scope

No new information surfaced during Epic 9 that changes #109's status. It remains **open**, root-cause-unknown, mitigated by the Linux-in-CI test skip (from PR #108, before Epic 9 started), and explicitly **not a blocker** for Epic 9 or Epic 4. Nothing to add here beyond confirming it hasn't quietly gone stale or been forgotten — it's referenced correctly in `sprint-status.yaml`'s `epic-9` note and in issue #110's closing comment.
